Five ways Made to Fit could look and feel

The current look (blue, coral measure tag, rounded grotesque) works, but it could belong to any app. Each direction below starts from something a nervous first-time builder already knows and trusts, then carries it through the whole product: the home screen, the mark, the colours, the type and one moment from inside the app.

Every board is drawn in that direction's own colours and fonts, so compare how each one feels, not the layout. Names, measurements and copy are real; the room photo is the one already on the site.

Direction 1Warm · cheerful · "anyone can do this"

The British TV craft make: cut card, sticky tape, flat bright colours and a finished one waiting under the table. It tells a first-timer the whole thing is a series of small, friendly steps, and that the result is something to be proud of.

The world

Flat card colours with no gradients or shadows except one hard "lift" under buttons. Shapes look cut out and taped down. Every screen ends with the finished piece.

Signature moment

The reveal. At the end of each step the parts on screen swap for the finished piece, like the one brought out from under the desk. The before/after slider on your photo becomes this reveal.

Why first-timers trust it

It makes the job feel small, warm and possible. It never looks like a tool for tradespeople.

Raised from other ideas

  • Every shape snaps to a 10 mm grid, so the playfulness still feels measured.
  • Measurements roll digit by digit when they change, so you see the number update.
  • One emblem per section (the taped cupboard) instead of scattered icons.

Watch out for

It can tip into children's TV. Keep the body type, measurements and safety notes plainly adult, and use the bright colours on surfaces, never on small text.

Reference

TV make segments, cut-paper illustration, Blue Apron's "Step 1 of 5" recipe cards (Mobbin).

Direction 2My pickPrecise · homely · grown-up

The pattern envelope

A dressmaking pattern is the original "made to fit": a numbered envelope with an "Easy" badge on the front, tissue pieces inside, every piece lettered, notched and laid out on the fabric to waste as little as possible. That is exactly what the cut list and sheet layout already do with a sheet of plywood.

The world

Envelope teal fields the size of the screen, tissue-coloured pieces with dashed cut lines, notches and grain arrows, one orange "Easy" badge. Square corners, no pills.

Signature moment

Draft the pattern. After the photo, pieces A to E slide out of the outline of your cupboard and settle onto the board, each lettered. The same letters run through the cut list, the order pack and the picture manual.

Why first-timers trust it

Patterns are made for amateurs and graded by difficulty. "Easy" and "1st build" on the front answer the real question before it is asked.

Raised from other ideas

  • Colour only where it means something: orange is the difficulty badge and the main button, nothing else.
  • Laid out like a garden plan: one straight axis from photo to pattern to finished cupboard.

Watch out for

Some people will read "sewing" before "joinery". The board, the mm and the roof angle have to stay on every screen so it reads as carpentry. It is also the most literal reading of the name.

Reference

Paper dressmaking pattern envelopes and tissue sheets, the existing cut list and sheet layouts, Parachute's quiet product pages (site) for how homely can still feel precise.

The workshop manual

The world

White paper, black line drawings, one red for arrows and callout letters. Bold condensed italic headings, numbered everything. Photos in a cool grey so the red arrows stand out.

Signature moment

The exploded view. On the home page and in the manual, the cupboard drawing comes apart into lettered panels as you scroll and goes back together at the end of the build.

Why first-timers trust it

It looks like the book that got people through their first brake job. The numbering says nothing is left out.

Raised from other ideas

  • Numbers as material: sizes are set large and tabular, never tucked into body text.
  • Every arrow is lettered and the letter appears in the step text.

Watch out for

It can read as a garage and as male. It also sits close to a famous manual's cover style, so the cover band and logo must stay our own.

Reference

Workshop manuals, appliance setup flows such as Fitbit's numbered fitting steps (Mobbin).

Direction 4Solid · honest · made of real stuff

The timber yard

The merchant's racks: stacked sheets, board ends painted in colour codes, stencilled grades and a ticket on every bundle. It says this is real material, cut properly, delivered to your door, and makes the order pack feel as solid as the board.

The world

Slate ground, ply-face panels with real grain, stencilled headings and one colour code per box painted on the board end. Colour appears only on the ends, as it does in a yard.

Signature moment

The colour code follows the piece. Box 1 is blue in the 3D preview, on the cut list, on the cutter's label and in the manual, so a first-timer can match parts by colour without reading a size.

Why first-timers trust it

It shows real material and a real supplier, which answers "will this actually turn up and fit?"

Raised from other ideas

  • Colour lives only at the edges of things, never as a page background.
  • Stencil type is for headings and labels only; running text stays a plain sans.

Watch out for

Of the five, this is the closest to "trade". Too much stencil and slate brings back the "Pro" problem the rename was meant to fix.

Reference

Merchant racks and board-end paint codes, Branch's warm material photography (site), Artek's wood-first pages (site).

Direction 5Personal · direct · it starts with your room

Marker sizes are ours; type you set is yours. Handwriting is never used for anything you need to read exactly.

The world

Your photo fills the screen; everything we add looks drawn on it in yellow marker, with red for questions and masking tape for labels. Plain heavy sans for the words. Dark by default so photos glow.

Signature moment

The markup becomes the design. The lines you draw on the photo tighten into the cupboard drawing in place, then the drawing fills in with doors and shelves on top of your room.

Why first-timers trust it

It starts from their own room, not from a product. Nothing on screen is abstract until they have seen it on the wall.

Raised from other ideas

  • The logo itself is a dimension: "to" between two marker brackets.
  • Handwriting only for marks and labels, so it never carries an exact number on its own.

Watch out for

It depends on photos. Pages with no photo (prices, help) need a strong plain fallback, and marker fonts can feel gimmicky if they spread.

Reference

The site's own A-frame loft photo, Zillow's staging slider already used in the app, Louis Poulsen's full-bleed hero (site).

My pick

The pattern envelope. It is the only direction where the brand and the product are the same idea: a pattern is pieces, lettered and laid out on a sheet to fit one person, which is exactly what Made to Fit makes from a photo. The "Easy · 1st build" badge speaks straight to a nervous first-timer, and the lettered pieces give the cut list, order pack and manual one shared language. The marked-up photo is the strongest alternative if you want the brand to be about the room rather than the making.

Directions can be mixed: the marked-up photo's measuring step works inside any of the other four.

Also on the table

  • The straight version. A clean home-improvement app in the style of the big DIY retailers' apps: white, one brand colour, product photography. Safe and familiar, and the most like today's site.
  • Flat poster illustration (1960s poster studios: flat candy colours, one bold silhouette per section). Held up on warmth but not on clarity; its "one emblem per section" rule is folded into direction 1.
  • Teletext pages, data-art barcodes, split-flap boards, cloud iridescence and formal garden plans were tried and dropped. What they lent: the measured grid and rolling numbers (1), large tabular sizes (3), colour only at edges (4), and the straight axis from photo to finished cupboard (2).

Researched with Inspo (24 furniture and home sites: the category leans on warm photography and grotesque sans, which is why none of the five uses that pairing) and Mobbin (step-by-step instruction screens). Fonts are free on Google Fonts and none is used by your other products.
